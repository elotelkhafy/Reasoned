Politics and the English Language

George Orwell · 1946

“The slovenliness of our language makes it easier for us to have foolish thoughts.”
Most people who bother with the matter at all would admit that the English language is in a bad way, but it is generally assumed that we cannot by conscious action do anything about it. Our civilisation is decadent and our language must inevitably share in the general collapse.

It follows that any struggle against the abuse of language is sentimental archaism. Beneath the familiar phrases and worn-out expressions, however, lies a practical problem: vague language can conceal vague thinking.

The great enemy of clear language is insincerity. When there is a gap between one's real and declared aims, people reach instinctively for long words and exhausted phrases.

RESPONSE A
“Orwell's argument is strongest when he connects vague language to vague responsibility. If we use abstract phrases, we can make choices sound inevitable rather than chosen. That does not mean language determines thought, but it can make certain thoughts easier to express and defend.”

What works? The writer distinguishes influence from determination.
What could improve? Give one concrete example from the essay.

RESPONSE B
“I agree with Orwell because people who use complicated words are usually trying to hide something. Simple language is always more honest.”

What works? Clear position.
What could improve? The claim “always” needs examination and evidence.
